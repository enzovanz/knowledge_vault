## Summary

An **externality** is a cost or benefit that an action creates for bystanders but that the decision-maker does not fully face. Externality charges, subsidies, tradable permits, or private agreements can bring those social effects into the decision, but each remedy has informational, administrative, and distributional limits.

## Explanation

With a negative externality, such as congestion or pollution, the private cost of an action is lower than its total social cost. People therefore tend to do more of it than they would if they faced the harm imposed on others. A positive externality reverses the problem: because the decision-maker does not capture every benefit, too little of the beneficial activity may occur.

### Making social effects part of the price

An externality charge, often called a **Pigouvian tax**, adds the marginal external damage to the price of an action. A subsidy can similarly reward an action that creates external benefits. The price provides both information about the social effect and an incentive to change behavior; it allows people to decide whether the action remains worthwhile instead of relying only on moral appeals.

The charge must apply at the relevant margin. An annual vehicle tax can affect car ownership, but it does not make the next journey more expensive. A per-trip congestion charge can influence whether, when, and where that journey happens. Ideally, the price varies with the harm: a trip on an empty road creates less congestion than one made at rush hour.

Tradable pollution permits use a related mechanism. The authority limits total emissions and permits can be exchanged, creating a price for scarce permission to pollute. Firms with cheaper ways to reduce emissions have an incentive to do so and sell their permits. The cap determines the allowed quantity, while trading helps reveal where reductions are least costly.

### Choosing a remedy

Government intervention is not always necessary. When the affected parties are identifiable, rights are clear, and negotiation and enforcement are inexpensive, they may reach a private agreement. Bargaining becomes impractical for congestion or climate change because vast numbers of people contribute to and experience the harm.

Public remedies can also fail. Governments may estimate costs and benefits poorly, set excessive charges or subsidies, incur administrative costs, or be influenced by organized interest groups. The practical choice is therefore between imperfect remedies: private bargaining, regulation, pricing, permits, or accepting some harm. A subsidy that exceeds the external benefit can produce too much of the subsidized activity.

### Efficiency and distribution

A policy can improve efficiency while distributing its costs unfairly. A road charge may be progressive where higher-income households drive more and lower-income households have good alternatives. The same policy may be regressive where lower-income workers must drive long distances and public transport is weak. The available alternatives and the use of the revenue are therefore part of evaluating the policy.

## Example

At rush hour, a driver considers the fuel and time required for a trip but may not face the delays imposed on every other road user, the added pollution, or the loss of safety and comfort for nearby residents. Free access does not eliminate scarce road space; it rations that space through congestion. A per-trip charge reflecting the additional harm makes the driver consider whether to travel then, choose another route or time, use another mode, or forgo the trip.
