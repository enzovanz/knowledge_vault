## Summary

Second-degree price discrimination offers customers a menu of prices and product conditions so they sort themselves by willingness to pay. The seller learns from their choices instead of needing to identify each customer's type beforehand.

## Explanation

The central constraint is **self-selection**: the cheap option must appeal to price-sensitive customers while leaving customers who value the premium features willing to pay more. If the cheap option delivers almost the same value, premium buyers can downgrade and undermine the pricing strategy.

Firms create this separation through:

- **Versions and quantities:** basic versus professional software, airline classes, subscription allowances, or bulk discounts.
- **Effort and flexibility:** coupons reward customers willing to search; advance-purchase, off-peak, or restrictive tickets attract buyers willing to adjust their plans.
- **Branding and convenience:** plain private-label packaging can distinguish a budget tier, while pre-packaged supermarket bundles can command a higher unit price from customers who value convenience.

These differences are not automatically price discrimination: larger seats, packaging, or handling can cost more to provide. The relevant question is whether the price differences also separate demand beyond what those costs explain.

One extreme is **damaged goods**: deliberately reducing a cheaper version's performance or functionality to protect the expensive version. Degradation can be profitable even if it saves no production cost, because it discourages premium customers from switching down. Product quality is then partly a tool for separating buyers.

## Example

Suppose a software firm offers a $20 basic plan and a $100 premium plan. If both provide the same useful features, customers have little reason to pay $100.

The firm limits batch processing in the basic plan. Occasional users tolerate the extra manual work, while heavy users pay more to save time. Even if enabling batch processing costs almost nothing, withholding it can sustain the price gap. The restriction works only if heavy users value the upgrade by at least the extra $80.
