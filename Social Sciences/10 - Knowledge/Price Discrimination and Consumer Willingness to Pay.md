## Summary

Price discrimination means charging different prices for the same or similar goods when supply costs do not explain the differences. **Price targeting** describes the strategies used to identify or separate customers according to their willingness to pay.

## Explanation

**Willingness to pay** is the most a customer would pay for a product. It depends on the value they receive and their alternatives, and is usually hidden from the seller. A single high price excludes some profitable customers; a low price gives discounts to customers who would have paid more.

The three degrees of price discrimination address this information problem differently:

| Degree | How customers are separated | Main limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **First: perfect individual pricing** | Charge each customer their exact maximum willingness to pay. | Requires information sellers rarely possess. |
| **Second: self-selection** | Offer a menu of versions, quantities, or conditions; customers reveal preferences through their choices. | Higher-paying customers may choose the cheaper option. See [[Second-Degree Price Discrimination and Self-Selection]]. |
| **Third: group pricing** | Use observable categories, such as student status, age, or location, to charge different prices. | Group membership is an imperfect proxy for individual demand. |

A student may be willing to pay more than a professional. Likewise, purchase history, browsing behavior, or location can help estimate individual demand without revealing an exact maximum price. Personalized pricing is therefore not automatically perfect discrimination.

The seller needs some pricing power and a way to keep prices separate. Product restrictions can discourage customers from choosing the cheap tier; eligibility checks can protect group discounts. Resale creates another problem: if buyers can purchase a medicine for $10 in one country and resell it in a $100 market, **arbitrage** undermines the price gap. Maintaining access at a low price can therefore depend on limiting resale into the expensive market.

## Example

Suppose each unit costs $10 to supply, and three customers would pay at most $100, $60, and $20. Each buys one unit if its price is no higher than their maximum.

| Pricing | Units sold | Revenue minus supply costs | Consumer surplus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| One price of $60 | 2 | $100 | $40 |
| Individual prices of $100, $60, and $20 | 3 | $150 | $0 |

**Consumer surplus** is willingness to pay minus the price paid. Perfect discrimination transfers the first buyer's $40 surplus to the seller and creates an additional $10 gain by serving the third buyer.

In this simplified model, every sale with willingness to pay above marginal cost occurs, but the seller captures all the gains. Actual price discrimination can expand access while reducing some buyers' surplus; its overall effect depends on output, product quality, and how the gains are shared.
