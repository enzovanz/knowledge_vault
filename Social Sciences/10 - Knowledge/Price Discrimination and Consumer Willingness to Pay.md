## Summary

Price discrimination is the practice of charging different customers different prices for the same or similar product based on their willingness to pay. In *The Undercover Economist*, Harford describes it as a way for firms to discover who values a product highly and who will only buy at a discount.

The price is not determined only by the cost of production. It is also shaped by how much value the customer receives, how many alternatives they have, and how easy it is for the seller to separate high-willingness-to-pay customers from low-willingness-to-pay customers.

## Explanation

The seller's problem is that customers privately know how much they are willing to pay. If the seller sets one high price, price-sensitive customers leave. If the seller sets one low price, high-willingness customers get a bargain and the seller leaves money on the table.

Price discrimination solves this by creating signals, fences, or product versions:

- **Group pricing:** student, senior, local, or off-peak discounts.
- **Versioning:** basic, premium, and luxury versions of a similar product.
- **Coupons and promotions:** discounts that mostly attract people willing to spend time searching.
- **Timing:** higher prices for urgent buyers and lower prices for flexible buyers.
- **Personalized offers:** digital platforms estimating willingness to pay from behavior, location, or history.

This works best when resale is hard. If discounted buyers can easily resell to full-price buyers, the separation collapses.

## Example

A supermarket may sell an ordinary product at full price while also offering coupons. The coupon is not mainly about lower production cost. It is a sorting mechanism. Customers who are price-sensitive spend time finding and using the coupon, while customers who care more about convenience pay the full price.

Both customers may buy the same product, but the seller captures more total revenue by charging closer to each customer's willingness to pay.
