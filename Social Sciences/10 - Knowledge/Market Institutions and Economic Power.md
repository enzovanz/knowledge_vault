## Summary

Markets do not exist independently of government and social institutions. Property rights, contracts, money, corporate law, competition policy, and enforcement define what can be traded and who has bargaining power, so “free market” describes a degree of constraint rather than an institution-free economy.

## Explanation

Competition makes price signals more useful because unusually high profits attract new suppliers. When entry is blocked, firms can preserve **market power**: the ability to raise prices, restrict supply, or dictate terms without quickly losing customers.

The barriers may be economic, such as high startup costs and network effects, or institutional, such as exclusive licenses and rules that favor incumbents. Even an online game's economy depends on code, administrator decisions, and enforceable ownership. If powerful guilds coordinate prices, they can create cartel-like scarcity inside those rules.

Regulation therefore has two opposing possibilities. It can protect competition, safety, and consumers; it can also be shaped by incumbents to make entry harder, a form of **regulatory capture**. The claim that AI companies support regulation solely to block competitors is a hypothesis, not a conclusion. It should be tested by examining specific proposals: Do they address a genuine harm? Are compliance costs proportionate? Do they apply equally? Could a smaller entrant realistically comply?

China illustrates that markets and state control are not opposites. Private exchange can coexist with state-owned firms, industrial policy, capital controls, and political direction over strategic sectors. The useful question is not whether an economy is simply “market” or “government controlled,” but which decisions use prices, which use administrative authority, and how those systems constrain each other.

## Example

### World of Warcraft auction-house cartels

*World of Warcraft* has a player-run auction house where sellers list virtual materials and crafted goods for gold. Groups of players have coordinated their purchasing, crafting, and listings to control particular server-level markets. With enough capital, they can buy the available stock, relist it at a higher price—a “market reset”—and keep watching the auction house to buy or undercut competing listings. One experienced player described his guild becoming active enough in one server-side market to control it for substantial periods.

The control did not last. Other players could farm or craft more of the item and list it below the guild's price, while continually buying competitors' supply depleted the guild's finite gold. The episode shows both sides of market power: coordination and concentrated resources can manipulate a market, but low barriers to entry make a cartel difficult to sustain. It also shows the institutional layer: Blizzard's auction-house design determines the market's geographic scope, listing rules, and ease of entry. When some commodities later became tradable across an entire region rather than within one server, the larger pool of competitors made coordinated control harder.

### AI regulation

A government may require AI systems to meet safety standards. If the rule defines measurable protections and offers a feasible compliance route for small firms, it may improve trust and competition. If it requires an enormous fixed approval cost that only established firms can bear, it may protect incumbents and manufacture scarcity even while being presented as a safety measure.
