## Summary

An interest rate is the price of moving purchasing power across time: borrowers pay to spend sooner, while savers are rewarded for postponing spending. The **nominal interest rate** is the stated rate; the **real interest rate** adjusts it for inflation and better measures the change in purchasing power.

## Explanation

For a rough calculation:

**real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate**

If a savings account pays 6% while prices rise 4%, its purchasing power grows by about 2%. The exact relationship is `(1 + nominal rate) / (1 + inflation rate) − 1`.

The real rate is not simply a separate “true” rate set by the market. Central banks directly influence short-term nominal policy rates. Market rates also reflect expected inflation, time horizon, credit risk, liquidity, and the supply of savings relative to demand for borrowing. Actual inflation then determines the realized real return; expected inflation helps determine the real return people anticipate when making a decision.

## Example

A loan charges 8% for one year. If prices rise 5% during that year, the lender receives 8% more currency but only about 2.9% more purchasing power. For the borrower, the real cost of bringing spending forward was therefore much lower than the stated rate.
