## Summary

Economic **efficiency** asks whether resources could be rearranged to make someone better off without making anyone worse off; **fairness** asks whether resources, opportunities, and burdens are distributed acceptably. A market can be efficient given its starting conditions while producing an outcome many people consider deeply unfair.

## Explanation

Under demanding assumptions, a perfectly competitive market uses prices to exhaust mutually beneficial trades. This is a benchmark, not a description of most real economies: it assumes conditions such as strong competition, adequate information, enforceable rights, and no unpriced effects on third parties.

Tim Harford's **Head Start Theorem** separates the market process from the starting distribution. In principle, society could first redistribute resources to give people an agreed “head start” and then let competitive exchange produce an efficient outcome. This suggests a division of labor: redistribution addresses fairness, while market prices coordinate later choices.

The result is more useful as a thought experiment than as a complete policy recipe. Governments cannot perfectly measure needs or make redistribution entirely neutral. Taxes and benefits can change behavior; political power shapes the chosen starting point; and many market failures remain. A theoretically perfect market plus a perfectly designed head start may be “as good as we can get” in the model, yet unattainable in practice.

## Example

Two runners can compete under identical rules, but calling the race fair is questionable if one starts far ahead. Moving both runners to an agreed starting line before the race preserves the competition while changing its distributional foundation. In an economy, cash, education, healthcare, or access to assets can serve as parts of that head start, though each changes incentives differently.
