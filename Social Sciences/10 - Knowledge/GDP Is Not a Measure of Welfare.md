## Summary

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** measures the market value of final goods and services produced within an economy during a period. It measures production, not overall welfare, so changes in GDP can diverge from changes in health, leisure, equality, environmental quality, and other parts of a good life.

## Explanation

GDP is useful for tracking market production, but it is not a complete balance sheet of social gains and losses. It can rise when resources are spent repairing damage, even though the original destruction made society worse off. Spending on long commutes or pollution cleanup contributes to production, while unpaid childcare, household work, leisure, and enjoyment of nature generally do not enter GDP directly.

This does not make the counted activities worthless or GDP useless. It means the measure answers a narrower question than “How well are people living?” Interpreting every increase in GDP as an increase in welfare confuses market activity with the value people ultimately receive from it.

Imports are subtracted when GDP is calculated using expenditure because consumption, investment, and government-spending figures can include goods produced abroad. The subtraction prevents foreign production from being counted as domestic production; it does not mean imports inherently reduce welfare.

## Example

A pollution charge may make a harmful activity more expensive and reduce its production. Measured GDP could fall, yet welfare could improve because people receive cleaner air and avoid damage that GDP did not subtract from production in the first place. Conversely, pollution followed by paid cleanup can increase measured activity without leaving society better off than if the pollution had never occurred.
