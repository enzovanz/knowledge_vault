## Summary

Scarcity is the fact that valuable things are limited relative to demand. In *The Undercover Economist*, Tim Harford uses scarcity to explain why some firms, locations, skills, and assets earn unusually high returns: they control access to something other people want but cannot easily reproduce.

Scarcity is not always natural. It can come from physical limits, regulation, timing, information, convenience, or deliberate restrictions. The important question is: **what exactly is scarce, and who captures the value created by that scarcity?**

## Explanation

Scarcity helps explain why prices and rents differ even when the visible product looks similar.

If a company has access to a scarce input, it can charge more than competitors who do not. If a landlord owns land near a busy station, the scarce thing is not the building itself but the location. If a coffee shop has the only convenient kiosk on a commuter route, the scarce thing is access to hurried commuters.

Harford's key move is to separate **cost** from **scarcity rent**:

- The cost of producing a cup of coffee might be low.
- The rent on a prime coffee kiosk might be high.
- The high rent exists because the location is scarce and customers are willing to pay for convenience.

Scarcity can also shift. A new train station, zoning rule, green belt, bridge, remote-work trend, or competitor can change which places and assets are scarce. When scarcity shifts, value moves with it.

## Example

A coffee cart inside a busy train station can charge more than an identical coffee cart several blocks away. The coffee is not necessarily better. The scarce resource is the station location: thousands of commuters pass by, many have little time, and only a few vendors can occupy that space.

The extra profit does not usually stay with the coffee seller forever. If the landlord understands how valuable the spot is, rent rises until much of the scarcity value is captured by the owner of the location.
