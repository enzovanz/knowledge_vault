## Summary

In a competitive market, a price both **rations a scarce good** and **communicates an incentive to supply more of it**. This makes prices useful coordination signals, but not neutral measures of need: demand reflects willingness **and ability** to pay.

## Explanation

A rising price performs two related functions:

1. It reduces current demand by excluding buyers who will not or cannot pay the higher price.
2. It tells producers that customers value additional supply, encouraging entry, investment, hiring, or higher wages.

If firms can enter and expand, the supply response can push the price back down over time. This mechanism depends on competition, usable information, enforceable rules, and the absence of barriers that prevent new supply. A high price may otherwise become a durable scarcity rent rather than a signal that successfully creates more output.

Prices also reveal only a particular kind of “truth”: what people will pay under the existing distribution of income and wealth. A rich person's minor preference can outweigh a poor person's urgent need in market demand. Efficient allocation and fair allocation are therefore different questions.

Non-market provision changes the rationing rule rather than eliminating scarcity. A public school may allocate places by residence instead of tuition, for example. If access to a desirable school raises demand for nearby homes, part of the school's value can be capitalized into property prices. Money then flows to property owners, and access may still favor wealthier families.

## Example

Suppose many parents want places at a highly regarded free public school. Because the school cannot immediately add places, families bid up nearby housing. The house price signals that the location and school access are scarce, but the payment goes to homeowners rather than directly funding more teaching capacity.
