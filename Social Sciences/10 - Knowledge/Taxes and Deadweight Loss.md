## Summary

A tax creates a **wedge** between what a buyer pays and what a seller receives. When that wedge prevents trades whose benefits exceeded their production costs, the lost gains from trade are called **deadweight loss**.

## Explanation

It is too strong to say that taxes destroy the information or “truth” carried by prices. Prices still convey information, but a tax changes the incentives facing both sides of the transaction. The buyer responds to the tax-inclusive price, while the seller responds to the amount retained after tax.

Some mutually beneficial trades then disappear. The government collects revenue on trades that still occur, but it collects nothing from a trade that the tax prevents. The value that neither buyer, seller, nor government receives is the deadweight loss.

This cost does not prove that all taxes are undesirable. Tax revenue can finance public services or redistribution, and taxes on harmful activities can make private decisions reflect social costs. The relevant comparison is between the tax's lost gains from trade and the value of what the policy accomplishes.

## Example

A cappuccino costs 60p before tax. A 10% tax raises the customer price to 66p, but one customer is willing to pay at most 63p. Without the tax, the sale occurs; with it, the sale disappears. No tax is collected, and the mutually beneficial trade between 60p and 63p is lost.
